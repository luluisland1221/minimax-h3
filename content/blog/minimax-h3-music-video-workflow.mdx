> **Quick answer:** Build a MiniMax H3 music video as a sequence of short, intentional shots. Divide the song into visual beats, define recurring character and color rules, use reference video only for authorized movement or camera rhythm, and generate 4–15 second clips with clear start and end states. Treat generated audio as a creative layer, then assemble and mix the final track in an editor.

A complete music video is longer than one MiniMax H3 generation. The model's documented 4–15 second output is better treated as a shot engine: performance inserts, narrative moments, transitions, visual metaphors, and loops are generated separately and assembled around a mastered song. This approach also makes failures cheaper to replace.

## Start with the song map

Before writing visual prompts, divide the track into sections:

| Song section | Visual job | Typical H3 shot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intro | Establish world and visual language | Wide environment or object motif |
| Verse | Introduce performer or narrative action | Medium performance and story inserts |
| Pre-chorus | Increase motion or anticipation | Moving camera, tighter cuts, rising light |
| Chorus | Deliver the strongest repeatable image | Hero performance, group movement, transformation |
| Bridge | Create contrast | New location, slow motion, abstract visual |
| Outro | Resolve motif | Return to opening object or controlled final frame |

Mark exact time ranges in the editing timeline. H3 prompts can describe approximate timing inside a clip, but the final synchronization to the mastered song should happen in editing.

## Choose a visual system

Write a one-page continuity guide before generation:

- Performer identity, hair, wardrobe, accessories, and makeup.
- Two or three recurring locations.
- Color palette and lighting rules.
- Camera rules for verse and chorus.
- Repeated object or visual motif.
- Aspect ratio and delivery resolution.
- Which scenes contain visible singing.
- Which audio comes from H3 and which comes from the master track.

Without these rules, individually attractive clips can feel like eight unrelated music videos.

## Prompt 3: abstract transition

> Five-second abstract music-video transition. Extreme macro of black ink and rose-colored metallic powder suspended in clear liquid. Begin with a still circular form. At one second, a bass-like pressure wave expands through the liquid and separates the colors into thin ribbons. At four seconds, the ribbons converge into a dark circular opening for the next shot. Photorealistic fluid behavior, deep black, crimson, muted violet, sharp specular detail. Add one low underwater pulse and fine granular movement, no melody, no text, no objects.

Abstract inserts are useful when performer identity is unstable or when two locations need a visual bridge. They should repeat the established palette rather than introduce a completely new style.

## Use reference video responsibly

A reference video can guide dance, gesture, performance energy, or camera movement. The prompt must say what to transfer and what not to copy. Use movement footage you created, licensed, or have permission to process. Do not assume a public performance clip is available for commercial motion transfer.

For a dancer, start with one identity image and one short movement reference. Ask H3 to preserve the identity and wardrobe from the image while following only the body timing from the video. Avoid complicated full-body choreography in the first test. Confirm anatomy and foot contact before adding camera motion.

## Audio strategy

There are three reasonable workflows:

1. **Master-track first:** generate visuals, discard most generated music, and edit to the licensed master.
2. **Performance reference:** use authorized audio to guide pace or singing performance, then replace the final mix.
3. **Sound-design layer:** keep useful H3 ambience and physical effects, then mix them under the song.

Never assume the generated soundtrack matches the key, tempo, arrangement, loudness, or rights requirements of the master. Keep stems organized and preserve a silent or effects-only version when possible.

## Shot and credit planning

A 60-second edit might use twelve five-second source clips, but real production needs alternatives. At 768P, one five-second H3 output uses 125 base credits on minimaxh3.pro. Twelve first attempts would use 1,500 credits before references; three variations per shot would use 4,500. Plan fewer hero shots, reuse successful loops, and test identity at four seconds before generating longer performances.

Use 2K selectively for close-ups, hero chorus shots, and clips that need cropping. A transition hidden under motion blur may not need the same resolution budget.

## Editing workflow

1. Build the song timeline and place markers.
2. Generate low-cost identity and movement tests.
3. Approve a continuity reference for performer and color.
4. Generate hero shots, then supporting inserts.
5. Label every clip with prompt version, seed if available, settings, and cost.
6. Cut visuals to the master track.
7. Mix selected generated effects beneath licensed audio.
8. Add exact titles, captions, credits, and disclosures in post.
9. Review identity, flicker, motion, lip sync, and rights before delivery.

## FAQ

### Can MiniMax H3 generate a full music video?

It generates short clips up to the documented duration. A full video should be assembled from planned shots in an editor.

### Can I upload a commercial song as audio reference?

Only upload audio you own or are authorized to process. Distribution and derivative-use rights require separate review.

### Should I keep MiniMax H3 generated music?

Keep it only when it fits the creative and rights requirements. Many productions use H3 effects or timing and mix a licensed master track in post.

### How do I keep the performer consistent?

Use controlled identity references, a continuity sheet, repeated wardrobe and lighting language, and shorter shots with intentional endpoints.
